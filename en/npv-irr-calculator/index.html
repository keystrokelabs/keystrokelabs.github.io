What NPV and IRR tell you

NPV is the value today of all the flows, each discounted at the rate you require. Zero or more means the project earns at least that rate. IRR is the rate at which NPV is exactly zero: the return the flows themselves give, per period. With yearly flows it is a yearly rate; with monthly flows, a monthly one.

Why a spreadsheet gives another NPV

A spreadsheet's NPV() treats its first value as one period away. With the initial investment inside the range, every flow is discounted one period too many. Enter the initial flow outside: =NPV(rate, CF1:CFn) + CF0. The table under the keys shows the number the wrong version gives for your flows.

When there is no IRR, or more than one

IRR needs money going both ways: at least one negative and one positive flow. If the signs change more than once (an investment, income, then a large cost), there can be two IRRs or none, and the calculators stop at the first one they find or report an error. NPV at your rate is the safer number then.

Common questions

How many cash flows can I enter?

This page takes up to 20 flows after CF0, each repeated up to 99 times, which is what the HP 12C holds. The BA II Plus and the HP 10bII+ hold more.

The calculator shows the IRR as a percent per period. How do I make it yearly?

With yearly flows, it already is. With monthly flows, the yearly nominal rate is the monthly IRR × 12, and the effective rate is (1 + IRR)^12 − 1.
